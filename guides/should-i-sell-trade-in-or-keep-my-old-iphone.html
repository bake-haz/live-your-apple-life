Should I Sell, Trade In, or Keep My Old iPhone?

There's no single right answer — but there is one right question: what is this phone worth to you right now?

You're probably here because you've got a new iPhone (or one is on the way), and the old one is sitting there with a question mark over it. Ads say trade it in. Friends say sell it privately for more. Family says keep it as a backup. You don't want to leave money on the table, but you also don't want to lose an afternoon to it — or, worse, hand over a phone full of your data. Let's make this decision calmly, in about the time it takes to read this page.
Direct answer: trade-in is the easiest, selling privately usually makes the most money, keeping it is fine if you have a real use for it, and giving it to family is good if you're willing to help them set it up. None of these is "correct." The right one depends on how much effort you want to spend, how much cash matters to you, and whether you have a use for a spare phone. Whichever you choose, the data step comes first and is the same: back up, sign out, erase.

What each option actually is

  • Trade-in. You hand the phone to a company (Apple's trade-in program, or your carrier) and get credit toward a purchase — usually toward the new iPhone you just bought. It's the least effort: you answer a few questions, they give a value, you send it or drop it off.
  • Selling privately. You list it yourself — on a marketplace, resale site, or to someone you know — and keep the full sale price. It typically returns more money than trade-in, and it costs you time, photos, descriptions, and a little patience with strangers.
  • Keeping it. The phone becomes a backup, a media player, a travel phone, or a test device. It costs nothing and risks nothing, as long as you actually use it for something.
  • Giving it to family. A parent, partner, or kid gets a perfectly good phone. The "price" is setup help and being the family tech support for it. For many people this is the best value of all — it just doesn't put cash in your pocket.

The real trade-offs: money, effort, and risk

Strip away the marketing and these are the actual differences:

  • Trade-in is the low-effort middle. The value is fixed before you commit, there's no negotiating, no shipping hassle in most cases, and no risk of a buyer disputing the sale. The trade-off is that the offered value is usually lower than what you'd get selling it yourself.
  • Private sale is the high-effort maximum. You can typically net more — sometimes meaningfully more — but you handle the listing, the meetup or shipping, and the small-but-real risk of scams or returns. If an afternoon of your time is worth more than the difference, private sale isn't a bargain.
  • Keeping it costs nothing but holds no cash. It's only a good deal if you have a use. An unused phone in a drawer is worth the same as no phone — it's just collecting dust.
  • Giving it away trades cash for goodwill. If someone close to you needs a phone, this can be the most satisfying option, and it sidesteps the whole resale process.

Timing matters too: phone values drop over time, and they tend to fall the most right after a new model is announced. If you're going to sell or trade, doing it while the phone is still current-ish usually beats waiting.

About the money (without pretending prices stay still)

We won't quote numbers, because trade-in values and resale prices change constantly — by model, condition, region, and season. What you need is the method:

  • Check today's trade-in value on Apple's trade-in page (or your carrier's). It takes two minutes and gives you a concrete baseline.
  • Compare a private-sale estimate. See what the same model and condition is actually selling for near you. If the gap is small, trade-in's convenience wins. If the gap is large and the phone is in good shape, private sale may be worth the effort.
  • Factor in condition honestly. A phone with a cracked screen or worn battery is worth notably less everywhere. If it's damaged, trade-in often becomes the sensible default — the private-sale premium shrinks fast.

You probably don't need to

  • Hunt for the absolute maximum. Chasing the last few dollars across platforms can eat an evening. A good-enough price you actually complete beats a perfect price you procrastinate on.
  • Keep it "just in case" forever. If you have no concrete use, an old phone in a drawer is worth nothing and quietly becomes e-waste. Decide, then act.
  • Buy accessories for it if you're keeping it as a spare. A backup phone doesn't need a new case or charger.
  • Worry that selling is dangerous. It's manageable — the key is doing the data steps below, and dealing with buyers through reputable channels with clear photos and an honest description.

The step that comes first, no matter what: your data

Before the phone changes hands — sold, traded, or given away — your data has to be off it. The safe sequence is:

  1. Make sure nothing important lives only on this phone. If you're not sure whether you're backed up, start with Do you need iCloud Backup? before you do anything else.
  2. Sign out of your Apple Account and other accounts on the phone (Settings > your name > Sign Out on newer iOS), so the next owner can't see or use them.
  3. Erase the phone the proper way, which also removes it from Find My. See When can I safely erase my old iPhone? for exactly when it's safe and how.

Do the erase after you've confirmed the new phone has everything. Erasing is the point of no return for that phone — but if you've backed up, it's a safe one.

A simple way to decide

Ask yourself one question: what do you actually want out of this phone?

  • You want it gone with zero effort → trade it in. Done in an afternoon, no negotiation.
  • You want the most cash and don't mind the process → sell it privately, with an honest listing and safe meeting habits.
  • Someone close to you needs a phone → hand it down. The value lands somewhere better than a resale listing.
  • You have a real use for a spare (travel, backup, kids) → keep it, guilt-free. A phone that's used isn't wasted.

Every option is legitimate. The only wrong move is letting the phone sit untouched while you keep postponing the decision.
