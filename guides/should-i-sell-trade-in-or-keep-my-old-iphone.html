Should I Sell, Trade In, or Keep My Old iPhone?

The old iPhone doesn't have one correct fate. The right choice matches what you value more than the phone itself.

You're here because the new phone works and the old one is still sitting there — and now the question feels uncomfortable, because every option is really a different trade. Selling means effort for money. Trade-in means less effort for a simpler transaction. Keeping means optionality, and also another device to store and charge. Giving it away means the value moves to someone else. None of these is "best" until you say what you want back.
Direct answer: there's no universally best option for an old iPhone. The choice depends on what you want back from it, how much effort you're willing to spend, whether the phone has a real future use for you, how much the highest possible sale value matters, and whether handing it to another person is worth more to you than selling it. Whichever direction you lean, the data step comes first and looks the same. What differs is the trade you're choosing.

Four goals, not four verdicts

Start by naming the goal — the option that fits follows from it:

  • I want the least hassle. Trade-in may fit, if the offered value is acceptable to you and the process suits how you'd rather spend your time.
  • I want to maximize cash. A private sale gives you more control over the asking price — and hands you the listing, the buyer questions, the negotiation, and the transaction risk that go with it.
  • I have a real use for the phone. Keeping it can make sense as a spare, a travel or emergency device, a media player, a child's device, or a test device.
  • Someone I know can genuinely use it. Giving it away can create more value for you than selling it — especially when helping another person matters more to you than cash.

These are goals, not rankings. Trade-in isn't automatically the right fit for "least hassle" any more than keeping is automatically wrong.

What each option actually is

  • Trade-in. A retailer, carrier, or trade-in service assesses the phone. The value depends on the current model, its condition, eligibility, and the program, and the offer changes over time. The transaction is simpler: you accept an offer, send or drop off the phone, and receive credit.
  • Private sale. You set the listing terms and handle the buyer's questions, negotiation, payment, and the handoff or shipping. You have more control over the price, and more of the work and buyer risk sits with you.
  • Keeping it. There's no transaction. The device stays available for another use — and it also stays as a thing to store, charge, and maintain, and as a software and account state that can need attention.
  • Giving it away. There are no sale proceeds. The value lands with someone you know, which can be meaningful when they need a phone. The recipient may need setup help and ongoing support from you.

No single option guarantees the highest financial outcome. That's decided by current offers, current market prices, condition, and your situation.

What are you optimizing for?

The useful question is what you're actually optimizing: money, time, convenience, usefulness, or helping someone else.

These goals pull against each other. The most money can cost the most time and effort. The least effort can mean accepting a lower offer. Keeping the phone preserves optionality and gives up the cash now. Giving it away trades financial return for meaning. Once you name the thing you're optimizing for, the option that fits stops hiding.

The cost of keeping it

Keeping the phone isn't free, and it isn't worthless — it carries an opportunity cost. The phone remains available as a backup, unavailable as cash, another device to store and charge, and another device whose software and account state may need attention from time to time.

Keeping is rational when the future use has real value to you — when you can name the trip, the emergency, or the job it would serve. If you can't name a foreseeable use, an honest reason to keep it is emotional: the comfort of having it around. That's a legitimate reason; it's just not a financial one. Don't dress it up as an investment claim.

Don't confuse value with resale price

Market value and personal value are different numbers. The examples below are hypothetical, to show the shape of the difference.

A phone worth a few hundred dollars in a resale market may be worth more to a parent who needs a working device for their child. The same phone may be worth less to its owner than the hours it would take to sell it. And a phone with low resale value can still be a useful emergency spare.

The resale price tells you what a market would pay. Your value also includes what the phone does for you, or for someone you care about. Both are real; they're just measured differently.

About the money

We won't quote numbers, because trade-in offers and resale prices change over time — by model, condition, region, and season. To compare fairly, gather two current data points and make one judgment:

  • The current trade-in offer for your exact model and condition.
  • Current completed sales of the same model and condition in your market — credible recent results, not wishful asking prices.
  • Your judgment: is the difference worth your time, the transaction effort, the shipping or meetup, and the inconvenience?

There's no market-timing rule to apply here. Offers and prices change over time, so use current market information when you make the decision.

The data step comes first

Before the phone changes hands — sold, traded, or given away — the new phone should already have taken over what you depend on, and the data you need should live somewhere you can reach it:

  1. Make sure nothing important lives only on this phone. If you're not certain your data is safe elsewhere, start with Do you need iCloud Backup?.
  2. Sign out and erase through the proper flow — sign out of your Apple Account, then use Erase All Content and Settings so your data is removed and, on a device that used Find My, Activation Lock is cleared for the next owner. When can I safely erase my old iPhone? walks through the safe order.

Don't manually delete photos or files while you're still signed in to iCloud — Apple notes that deletions made on a signed-in device propagate to iCloud and your other devices. The proper erase flow is the clean way to let go.

You probably don't need to

Chase the absolute highest offer if the extra money isn't worth your time. Keep the phone merely because it still turns on. Buy accessories for a device you're about to sell or trade. Sell immediately just because a new iPhone arrived — the decision is about your goals, not the calendar. Or treat giving a phone away as wasting money when helping someone is the value you actually wanted.

A simple way to decide

I want the least effort → compare the current trade-in offer with the amount of effort you avoid.

I want the most control over price → a private sale may fit, provided the expected extra value is worth the extra work and transaction risk.

I have a genuine use for a spare → keep it — and name the use. If you can't name one, the case for keeping it is weak.

I want this phone to help someone else → give it away, after the same safe data and security handoff.

One check before you commit

Ask which regret would bother you more:

  • "I could have received more money."
  • "I spent too much time selling it."
  • "I got rid of a useful spare."
  • "I kept a phone I never used."
  • "I could have helped someone with it."

The regret you'd least want to live with points at the goal you actually care about.

The old iPhone isn't a problem you need to get rid of. It's an asset whose next job you need to choose. Money, convenience, usefulness, or helping someone else — once you know which one matters most, the decision gets much smaller.
