Why Does an App Still Seem to Work After I Closed It?

Closing an app and stopping an app are two different things.

You're probably here because you swiped an app up from the app switcher to close it, then later got a notification from it, heard it playing audio, or saw it had updated something. It feels like the phone ignored you. It didn't. "Closed from the switcher" and "fully stopped" are not the same action on iPhone.
Direct answer: closing an app from the switcher mostly just removes its open window — it doesn't stop the things iOS is allowed to keep doing for you. Notifications, incoming calls, audio, location, and scheduled refreshes are handled by the system, not by the open app. Those keep working by design. That's usually convenient, not a bug. You can turn most of them off per app if you'd rather they didn't.

What "closing" an app actually does

When you open the app switcher and swipe an app away, you end its foreground session. The app is no longer on screen and isn't actively using the processor for what you were doing. But iOS keeps several capabilities alive on purpose, because they're useful:

  • Notifications. These arrive through Apple's push system, not through the app sitting open. An app can notify you whether or not its window is on screen.
  • Incoming calls and messages. The Phone and FaceTime apps, and messaging apps, receive these through the system even when "closed."
  • Audio. If an app was playing something (music, a podcast, a video call), iOS lets it continue in the background until you stop it — that's why audio sometimes keeps going after you leave the app.
  • Location. An app you granted "While Using" or "Always" location can keep receiving location in the background for navigation or reminders.
  • Scheduled refreshes. With Background App Refresh on, apps can quietly fetch new content so it's ready when you next open them.

None of that means the app is "still running like it was." It means a small, system-managed slice of it is allowed to do specific jobs. Think of closing the app as closing the window, not unplugging the phone.

Why this feels wrong (and usually isn't)

On a computer, closing a window often ends the program. On iPhone, the model is different: the OS decides what's allowed to keep living so your experience is smooth. Three results people notice:

  • "It notified me even though I closed it." Expected — notifications don't need the app open.
  • "Music kept playing." Expected — audio continues until you pause it in Control Center or the Lock Screen.
  • "It used data while I wasn't looking." Could be a background refresh or an app finishing a sync. See Why does my iPhone seem to do things on its own?

This design is also why a warm phone in your pocket often isn't broken — background activity from "closed" apps is one of the normal causes. See Why does my iPhone get warm when I'm not using it?

A simple way to decide what to control

You don't have to manage all of this. Pick by annoyance:

  • If notifications bug you — turn them off per app in Settings > Notifications. The app still works; it just stays quiet.
  • If background data or battery bugs you — look at Settings > General > Background App Refresh and switch individual apps off. They'll only refresh when you open them.
  • If location bugs you — in Settings > Privacy & Security > Location Services, change each app to "While Using" or "Never."
  • If audio won't stop — pause it from Control Center or the Lock Screen. That's the real "off" switch for sound.

When closing the app is the right move

Closing from the switcher is genuinely useful in a few cases — it's just not a daily necessity:

  • An app is frozen or misbehaving. Swiping it away and reopening clears a stuck state.
  • An app is doing something you want to stop now. For audio or a live session, closing it ends the foreground part immediately (though you may still need to pause audio separately).
  • You're troubleshooting. Support steps often start with "close and reopen the app" because it resets the session.

What closing does not do: free meaningful battery in normal use, or "kill" notifications. If battery drain is your real worry, the Battery screen tells you which app is actually responsible — see Why does my iPhone battery drop so fast after I get it?

The "too many apps open" worry

A common fear is that a long app switcher means twenty apps are "running" and slowing the phone or draining the battery. That's not how iOS works. Apps you've left in the switcher are mostly suspended — held in memory but not using the processor. They sit there like open tabs you haven't clicked, not like programs spinning in the background.

  • Suspended apps use almost no power. They're frozen until you return to them. They don't tick, fetch, or compute while suspended.
  • Having many suspended apps doesn't slow the phone. When you open a new app and memory is tight, iOS quietly pushes the oldest suspended one out of memory. That's automatic; you don't manage it by hand.
  • Closing them all can backfire. Reopening a cold app takes more energy than waking a suspended one, so a daily "close everything" habit can slightly increase, not decrease, battery use.

The one exception is an app that's genuinely misbehaving — frozen, stuck, or looping. For that single app, swiping it away is the right move, because you're clearing a broken session rather than tidying a healthy one.

You probably don't need to

  • Close every app every day. It doesn't save battery and can even cost a little, because reopening apps later takes more power than leaving suspended ones alone.
  • Worry that the phone "ignored" you. It carried out exactly what closing does. The rest is system behavior you can adjust.
  • Assume an app is spying because it notified you. Notifications are the app asking the system to alert you — not the app running hidden on your screen.
