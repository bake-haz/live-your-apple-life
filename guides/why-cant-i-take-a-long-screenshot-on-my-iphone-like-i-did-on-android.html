Why Can't I Take a Long Screenshot on My iPhone Like I Did on Android?

Your iPhone can capture a whole page — but mostly only web pages. Everywhere else, it grabs one screen, on purpose.

You're probably here because on Android you'd scroll, hit screenshot, and get the entire conversation or article in one tall image. On iPhone you press the buttons, and you get exactly one screen — the part you were looking at, cut off mid-scroll. It feels like a downgrade, and for a lot of everyday moments it is a real limitation. The good news: there's a built-in "full page" capture, and it covers the case people miss most — web pages.

Direct answer: iPhone takes a normal one-screen screenshot by default, just like a photo of whatever is on display. A built-in "full page" option exists, but it's offered mainly for web content viewed in Safari — it captures the entire page as one long image or a PDF. For most other apps, iPhone doesn't stitch a scrolling screenshot together, so you get a single screen. The fix isn't a hidden setting; it's knowing when full page is available and when to use "save" or "share" instead of "screenshot."

How screenshots work on iPhone

To capture the screen, press the side button and the volume-up button at the same time (on older models with a Home button, it's side or top plus Home). A small thumbnail appears in the bottom-left corner. If you ignore it, the picture is saved to Photos. If you tap it, an editor opens where you can crop, mark up, or — for some content — capture more than one screen.

The "Full Page" option, and where it appears

When you tap the screenshot thumbnail and open the editor, you may see a choice between the standard capture and a "Full Page" version. For a web page opened in Safari, Full Page captures the whole article or site from top to bottom as a single tall document. You can then save it as a PDF (often the default for full page) or as an image, depending on the option shown.

The important part: this full-page capture is tied to web content. It's most reliable inside Safari for pages you're viewing. For many other apps, the Full Page choice simply isn't offered, because the app hasn't given the system a scrollable document to stitch together.

Why most apps only give one screen

On Android, many apps built scrolling-capture directly into their share or screenshot flow, so a long chat or list came out whole. iPhone leaves that decision to each app and to the system, and the system only offers full-page stitching for certain content types — chiefly web pages. So a long message thread, a tall note, or a multipage document inside an app will usually produce just the visible screen. It's not that your iPhone "can't;" it's that the built-in tool only automates full-page capture for web views, and other apps haven't added their own version.

What to do instead of a long screenshot

Depending on what you're trying to keep, a screenshot may not be the best tool:

  • For a web page: use Full Page and save as PDF. You get the entire page in one file, searchable and clean.
  • For something in an app: look for the app's own "export," "save," or "share" option. Many apps can produce a PDF or a shareable link that's better than a cropped image of the screen.
  • For a chat or thread: some messaging apps let you save or export a conversation directly. That keeps the whole thing instead of a dozen separate screenshots you later stitch by hand.
  • For a document: share or export the original file rather than photographing it. The text stays selectable.

In short: when the content already exists as data, saving or sharing that data beats screen-capturing it.

Image or PDF — which to save

When Full Page is offered, you'll usually choose between saving as an image and saving as a PDF. An image is easy to preview and send in a message; a PDF keeps the whole page as one tidy document you can open anywhere and often search later. For a long article you want to keep, PDF is usually the better pick. Beyond Safari, some of Apple's own apps let you export an entire item as a PDF through their share or print option — Notes, Mail, and Maps, for example, can turn a whole note, message thread, or map area into one PDF. That's the closest built-in equivalent to Android's scrolling capture for content that lives in an app: you're not screenshotting; you're exporting the real thing.

Crop to just what matters

Full Page is great for "the whole thing," but often you only need a slice — a confirmation, a map, one paragraph. In that case the ordinary screenshot is the right tool, and the editor that opens when you tap the thumbnail lets you crop and mark up before saving: draw a circle, underline text, hide sensitive numbers with the marker. Cropping also keeps the file small and readable on a chat, where a ten-screen PDF would be overkill. So the real choice isn't "long vs short" — it's "do I need the entire page, or just point at one part?" Pick the smaller capture when the smaller capture says enough.

You probably don't need to worry about

Your screenshot button being "broken." It's working exactly as designed — one press, one screen. You also don't need a third-party scrolling-capture app to survive daily life; for the most common case (web articles) Full Page already covers you, and for everything else, save or share the real content. Installing an extra app mostly trades one limitation for a new privacy question about what that app can read.

A simple way to decide

Ask "is this a web page or already a file?" If yes, capture or export the whole thing (Full Page for web, share/export for files). If it's only on screen inside an app with no export, accept the single screenshot — or use the app's own save — rather than hunting for a system setting that isn't there.
