Why Won't My Bluetooth Device Connect to My iPhone?

Before you reset anything, find out whether the problem follows the iPhone or the Bluetooth device.

You're here because a Bluetooth device that should connect isn't connecting — and it's natural to wonder whether the iPhone's Bluetooth is broken. One failed connection is information about one connection. It isn't automatically a diagnosis of the phone.
Direct answer: find out whether the failure follows the iPhone or the device. The fastest useful test is to try a different Bluetooth device. If another Bluetooth device connects, the iPhone's Bluetooth path is working, and the problem is narrower — it points at the original device or its pairing. If several known-working Bluetooth devices all fail to connect, the evidence points more toward the iPhone side and is worth investigating further. Don't start by resetting the phone; start by seeing which side the failure follows.

The fastest useful test

Try a different Bluetooth device. The value of this test isn't that it "fixes" Bluetooth — it's that it tells you whether the problem travels with the iPhone. Put a second device (a keyboard, another speaker, a different pair of headphones) into pairing mode and see if the iPhone finds and pairs it. One result changes where you spend your effort:

  • Another device works → the iPhone's Bluetooth path is functioning. The issue is the original device or its pairing relationship.
  • Several devices fail → the evidence points more toward the iPhone side — Bluetooth state, recent software, or the wireless stack — and that's the place to look next.

This single test replaces a lot of guessing. It doesn't repair anything by itself; it locates the layer where the failure lives.

Bluetooth is a connection between two devices

A Bluetooth link is a handshake between the iPhone and the accessory. Both sides have to cooperate, so the check has to look at each side separately.

iPhone side

  • Bluetooth is turned on in Settings > Bluetooth.
  • The iPhone is running current software where an update is appropriate.
  • A restart has been tried when the connection is stuck.
  • Device-specific settings, such as audio routing, are set the way you want.

The other device side

  • It's powered on and charged enough to pair.
  • It's in discoverable or pairing mode — its own step, not the iPhone's.
  • It's close to the iPhone, with a clear path between them.
  • It isn't already connected to another phone or laptop.
  • If it supports a pairing reset (Apple or the maker), that reset is device-specific — there is no single universal button.

Four states, four different next steps

The same words "it won't connect" hide four different failures. Name the state you're in:

A. The device doesn't appear. The iPhone can only see a device that is actively in pairing mode. Check discoverable mode, distance, the device's power, and that Bluetooth is on. The device simply isn't broadcasting yet.

B. It appears but pairing fails. This points at the pairing itself — an existing pairing on one side, a compatibility limit, or a code or PIN prompt that was ignored. Clear the old pairing and try again.

C. It pairs but disconnects. Once paired, the link depends on distance, interference, the device's own state, and its battery. Move them closer and confirm the accessory is awake and charged.

D. It pairs and works briefly, then stops. Now determine whether the failure follows the device or the iPhone. If it happens with this device only, the device is the clue; if it happens across several devices, the iPhone side is the clue.

Forget This Device is a way to clear a pairing

When a device paired before but won't reconnect, the old link is what's stuck. On the iPhone, open Settings > Bluetooth, tap the information button next to the device, and choose Forget This Device. Then put the accessory back into pairing mode and pair again.

This is a way to clear an old pairing relationship — not proof that Bluetooth itself is broken. It clears the link on the iPhone; if the accessory also "remembers" the old pairing, clear it on the accessory too (per its manual), then pair fresh from both sides. Don't run this on every device as a routine; use it for the one connection that's actually stuck.

What the test tells you

  • One device fails, others work → look at that device and its pairing.
  • Several devices fail → investigate the iPhone side.
  • It appears but won't pair → pairing, compatibility, or an existing pairing state.
  • It pairs but keeps dropping → find out whether the behavior follows the device or shows up across multiple devices.

Before you blame your iPhone

A Bluetooth device that suddenly won't connect doesn't prove the whole iPhone has a problem. One failed connection is information about one connection. It isn't automatically a diagnosis of the phone. The calm move is to test one more device before assuming the radio is at fault — because a single stubborn accessory looks identical to a broken phone until you check.

If it's only your car

If only the car fails while other Bluetooth devices connect fine, don't start by suspecting the iPhone's Bluetooth hardware. A car has its own stored pairing list, its own discovery mode, and its own profile state. First delete the old pairing from the car, put the car back into its pairing mode, and confirm the car is currently allowing a new device to join. The exact steps differ by make and model, so follow the car maker's instructions rather than a generic rule — the iPhone is just waiting for the car to invite it.

AirPods have their own guide

If the device you're struggling with is AirPods, they have their own dedicated guide: Why won't my AirPods connect to my iPhone? The steps below are for generic Bluetooth devices — keyboards, mice, speakers, controllers, car audio, and headphones that aren't AirPods.

You probably don't need to

Don't reset the whole iPhone because one earbud, keyboard, or car won't connect. Don't delete every paired device at once as a first step. Don't switch off privacy or wireless settings to scrape together a fix. Don't turn one device's problem into "the iPhone's Bluetooth is broken." And don't assume the hardware is faulty the first time a single pairing fails — test another device first.

When several devices fail on the iPhone

If the cross-device test shows the iPhone side is the problem, work light to heavy. Confirm Bluetooth is on, restart the iPhone, and install any available software update. Bluetooth shares the iPhone's wireless hardware with Wi-Fi; when several wireless features misbehave together after an update, that points to the radio state, and a restart is the first step.

Reset Network Settings is a last resort, not a Bluetooth diagnosis. Use it only when several Bluetooth devices fail, the lighter steps above haven't helped, and the problem clearly sits in the iPhone's connection state. It resets Wi-Fi network and password memory, cellular settings, VPN and APN configuration, and Bluetooth pairing info — so it costs more than the pairing alone. Reach for it only after the cross-device test points at the iPhone.

A simple way to decide

Only one device fails → start with that device and its pairing state.

Several known-working Bluetooth devices fail → investigate the iPhone side.

The device appears but won't pair → pairing, compatibility, or an old pairing state.

It pairs but keeps dropping → test another device and see whether the failure follows the device.

Only your car fails → look at the car's stored pairing and its pairing mode.

You don't need to troubleshoot Bluetooth everywhere. You only need to find out whether the problem follows the iPhone or the device.
