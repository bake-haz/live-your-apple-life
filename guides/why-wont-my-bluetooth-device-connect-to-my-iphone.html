Why Won't My Bluetooth Device Connect to My iPhone?

Bluetooth pairing is mostly about the other device — not the iPhone.

You're probably here because you put your headphones, car, or speaker into pairing mode, tapped around on the iPhone, and nothing happened — or it paired once and now won't reconnect. It's tempting to blame the phone. But Bluetooth pairing is a two-device handshake, and usually the part that's stuck is on the accessory's side.
Direct answer: the iPhone is rarely the broken half. For a new device, the accessory must be in its own "pairing" or "discoverable" mode — a step people skip. For a device that paired before, the fix is usually to "forget" it on the iPhone and pair again. Get those two right and most pairing problems disappear.

How pairing actually works on the iPhone

Open Settings > Bluetooth. When Bluetooth is on, the iPhone broadcasts that it's looking, and a list of nearby devices in pairing mode appears under "Other Devices." Tap one to pair. That's the whole flow. The catch: the iPhone can only see a device that is actively in pairing mode — a headphone that's just on, or a car that's just running, often isn't broadcasting. Each accessory enters pairing mode its own way (hold a button, say "pair," toggle a setting), and the iPhone has no control over that half.

For a device you've never paired

  • Put the accessory in pairing mode. Check its manual or the maker's site for the exact gesture. Until it's in that mode, it won't show up — and that's not an iPhone fault.
  • Keep them close. A few feet apart, with a clear line, pairs far more reliably than across the room or through a bag.
  • Watch for the confirm code. Some devices show a code on both screens; approve it on the iPhone. If you ignore that prompt, pairing silently fails.
  • Only one connection at a time. Most headphones or speakers connect to a single phone. If they're still linked to a laptop, they may refuse the iPhone until you disconnect the first.

For a device that paired before but won't reconnect

This is the common one. The old link is stuck, or the accessory "remembers" a different phone. On the iPhone, under Settings > Bluetooth, tap the i next to the device and choose Forget This Device. Then put the accessory back into pairing mode and pair fresh. It feels like a reset, but it's really just clearing a confused memory on both sides.

If it still won't hold, restart the iPhone and the accessory. A restart clears the temporary Bluetooth state that sometimes refuses to reconnect after an update or a long time asleep.

You probably don't need to

Don't reach for "Reset Network Settings" as your first move for a Bluetooth problem — it clears Wi-Fi and cellular settings too, and is rarely needed for pairing. Don't assume the iPhone's Bluetooth is defective either; a phone that pairs with one accessory but not another is telling you the issue is the stubborn accessory, not the radio.

When something else is in the way

A few ordinary things interfere: the accessory is already connected to another phone in your pocket; it's out of charge (some won't pair while nearly dead); or it's a car that only pairs when the engine is on and the audio screen is up. Physical obstruction and other Bluetooth devices nearby add minor noise but rarely block pairing entirely. If a specific car keeps failing, the car's own Bluetooth list may be full and need its oldest entry deleted.

If audio stutters or cuts out

Once paired, Bluetooth audio can still drop in and out. The usual reasons are distance and obstruction — Bluetooth reaches a few meters, and a body or wall between phone and earbuds weakens it. Other active Bluetooth devices and some Wi-Fi congestion add minor interference. If it only happens at the far end of the room, that's range, not a fault. An accessory with a nearly dead battery may also drop connection to save power. None of these mean the iPhone's Bluetooth is broken; they're the normal limits of a short-range radio.

Cars: the usual gotchas

Car pairing trips up more people than any other case. Most cars only pair while the engine is on and the audio or phone screen is up, and many remember a limited number of phones — if yours is "full," delete the oldest entry in the car's Bluetooth list first. Also, a car already linked to a different phone in the car will ignore your iPhone until that link is dropped. These are car rules; the iPhone is just waiting for the car to invite it, and no iPhone setting forces a stubborn car to cooperate.

Forget it on both sides

When a reconnect is stubborn, forgetting the device only on the iPhone sometimes isn't enough — the accessory may still "remember" the old pairing and refuse the new one. If the accessory lets you clear its pairings (a long-press or a reset button, per its manual), do that too, then pair fresh from scratch. Starting clean on both ends resolves more cases than people expect, especially for headphones that were previously tied to another phone.

One more thing: the radios are linked

Bluetooth shares the iPhone's wireless hardware with Wi-Fi and with AirDrop. If all three misbehave at once after an update, that points to the radio stack, and a restart or — as a last resort — Reset Network Settings tends to clear it. When only Bluetooth fails, it's the accessory; when all wireless fails together, it's the phone's state. That distinction saves a lot of guessing before you touch any settings.

A simple way to decide

If the iPhone pairs with other Bluetooth devices but not this one → the accessory is the stuck half; forget and re-pair it, or check its pairing mode. If the iPhone pairs with nothing → restart it, then, as a last step, Reset Network Settings. That split tells you where to spend your effort.
